Which cut for what

A handful of cuts covers nearly everything in whittling. Find what you're trying to do, then read the cut.

The cuts

From the first to learn to the last. Easy cuts work on day one. Takes practice cuts need a few tries. Tricky ones fight the grain partway through.

Reading the grain

Wood is a bundle of fibers running along the grain, like a bundle of drinking straws. Carving blocks have the grain along their longest side, and the planner tells you which way it runs in your block. Most of what feels hard about a cut is the grain.

Cut downhill

On a curve, cut from the high point down toward the low point. The blade then slices across fiber ends as they run out of the surface, and the shaving comes off clean.

Cut the other way (uphill) and the blade follows fibers that dive into the wood. It digs in, and the wood tears or splits ahead of it. If the wood tears, turn the piece around and come from the other side.

Short grain

Where a thin part runs across the grain, its fibers are short and nothing holds them together: an ear, a bill, the rim of a cap. It snaps off along the grain. The planner's grain check highlights these spots.

Leave short-grain parts thick until last, support them with a finger while you cut, and cut toward the body rather than away from it.

End grain

The ends of the fibers: the top of a figure that stands with the grain up, or the nose and tail of a long one. End grain is hard and crushes instead of slicing.

Cut it with a freshly stropped knife, in thin push cuts that slice across the fibers at an angle.

Slice, don't push

Draw the blade along its length as it moves forward, the way you slice bread. A slicing cut takes less force, leaves a smoother surface, and makes the biggest difference in end grain and hollows.

What makes a design hard

The levels in the planner come from these. Beginner designs are outward curves or flat planes with nothing thin. Easy ones add a neck, a hollow or a few gaps. Intermediate ones have thin parts and narrow gaps.

Thin parts
Ears, tails, bills and flukes break, especially when they run across the grain. Sitting cat's ears, whale's flukes, the toadstool's cap.
Gaps and inside corners
Between legs or between figures there's no room to swing the knife. You need stop cuts and lever cuts, working from both sides. Walking bear, dachshund, ducks in a row.
Hollows
A dished face or the underside of a chin needs scoop cuts, and the grain reverses halfway across every hollow. The owl's face, under the toadstool's cap.
Undercuts
Anything that hangs over: an ear that hangs free, a hat brim, a cap. You're reaching underneath, and it's usually short grain too. Dachshund's ears, gnome's brim, toadstool.
Matching pairs
Two eyes, two ears and four legs have to match. Redraw the centerline after every step, and work both sides in turn, a few cuts each.
Big smooth curves
Every bump and stray facet shows on a smooth surface. Rustic, flat-plane designs are more forgiving, because nothing has to blend.
Easiest of all
Chunky shapes that curve outward, with the grain along the longest part. Penguin, loaf cat, turtle, the rustic designs.

    A better knife

    When it's time

    • It won't get sharp, or it loses its edge minutes after stropping.
    • The blade is too long to steer the tip into small details.
    • The handle hurts after half an hour.
    • It folds, and the blade wobbles or could close on your fingers.

    What to look for

    • Fixed blade. No folding.
    • About 1 to 1 1/2 in of blade for figures this size, with a straight edge and a fine point.
    • A flat bevel (a "Scandi grind") that lies flat on a strop, so it's easy to keep sharp.
    • Carbon steel sharpens easily. Wipe it dry, because it can rust.
    • Sold razor-sharp, ready to carve.
    • A handle that fills your hand: oval, not round, so it doesn't turn in your grip.

    The kinds of knife

    Bench or cutting knife
    About 1 1/2 in of blade. The all-rounder that does most of every carving. Start here.
    Detail knife
    A shorter, pointed blade for eyes, tight corners and lever cuts. A good second knife.
    Sloyd knife
    About 3 in of blade, for roughing out bigger blocks like the 4 × 2 × 2 quickly.
    Hook knife
    A curved blade for deep hollows. Mostly for spoons and bowls; small figures rarely need one.

    Knives carvers often recommend: Flexcut's cutting knife (KN12) and detail knife (KN13), and the Morakniv 120, a short laminated-steel blade that doesn't cost much. The Mora 106 is its longer cousin, for roughing out. Small makers sell hand-made carving knives at higher prices. Reviewers disagree about some of these, so read a few current reviews before you buy.

    One good knife and a strop beat a set of six.

    Keeping it sharp

    A sharp knife is the safe one: it needs little force, so it doesn't slip.

    1. Rub honing compound into a leather strop.
    2. Lay the bevel flat on the leather and pull the knife backward, spine first, with the edge trailing. Never push it edge first, or it cuts the leather.
    3. Ten to twenty strokes on each side.

    Strop every 20 to 30 minutes of carving, and whenever cuts start to take more force or the surface looks fuzzy. You only need a sharpening stone when stropping no longer brings the edge back.

    Test it: a sharp knife slices paper cleanly and leaves a shiny cut on end grain. A dull one crushes the wood and leaves white fuzz.

    Safety

    • Before each cut, ask where the blade goes if it slips, and keep every part of you out of that path.
    • Wear a cut-resistant glove on the hand that holds the wood, and a thumb guard or a wrap of tape on your knife hand's thumb for pull cuts.
    • Sit down, brace your elbows against your body or knees, and use short strokes.
    • Rest when your hands get tired. Most slips come from forcing a dull knife.
    • Never try to catch a falling knife. Let it drop.
    • Keep a few bandages nearby.

    When something goes wrong

    The wood tears or splits ahead of the bladeYou're cutting uphill, against the grainTurn the piece around and cut from the other end. See cut downhill.
    The surface is fuzzy, whitish or crushedThe knife is dullStrop it, and slice instead of pushing straight.
    A chunk split off past your lineThe cut ran on along the grainMake a stop cut on the line first, and take thinner bites.
    A thin part snapped offShort grainGlue it back with wood glue or thin superglue and hold it with a rubber band until it sets. Next time, leave it thick until last.
